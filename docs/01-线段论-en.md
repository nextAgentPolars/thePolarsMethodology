# Segment Theory (线段论)

**1985, Tom Cargill at Bell Labs told a joke: the first 90% of the code takes 90% of the time, and the remaining 10% takes another 90%.**

Forty years later, the joke is still being told—and it has only grown more relevant. In an age where a single sentence can generate a demo, "starting" has never been cheaper, and "arriving" has never been rarer. Sixty percent of projects never reach production, and the words "it's done" are usually declared by the same people who wrote the code.

The problem isn't the tools. It's not capability, either. There's only one root cause: development has lost sight of its own destination.

The destination used to be almost laughably simple—a concrete artifact. An interface you can open. A command you can run. The Segment Theory is an attempt to pull all of development work back onto that line.

---

## I. A Task Is a Line Segment

Think of it like second-grade math:
A line segment has three parts:
Endpoint A,
Endpoint B,
and the straight line in between.

That's it.

At a sufficiently high level of abstraction, every development task is just a line segment:
a starting point, the development process in between, and the delivered result.

In the past, we used to ask how big or complex something was.
Here, we don't. We only need three questions answered:

- How does the starting point legitimately enter?
- How does the destination legitimately connect?
- And what is the main trunk that keeps moving forward in between?

#### The entry must be specified clearly:

What are the preconditions? Who receives the task, and what state are they in when they take it? The exit must also be specified clearly:

What counts as done? Who does it get handed to? And if it fails, in what manner does it fail?

The middle is the trunk—the line that moves continuously from entry to exit. It is not a collection of parallel feature points.

Note:
There is nothing new here.
No feature list.
No module diagram.
No code size estimates.

From a sufficiently abstract perspective, every project you've ever worked on is just this one line:

**The outermost line is the project itself. Both ends are locked and unchangeable. Everything inside grows recursively from it.**

---

The functional perspective of the past wasn't wrong. In traditional development, it didn't bother us much—or we didn't notice it. It wasn't a problem per se; it just tended to pull people into local views.

AI has sharply amplified this weakness.

Models generate code far faster than humans can review it. And the model is only responsible for its own output—not for your trunk.

When you use agent tools like Claude Code or Codex, their point capabilities are genuinely strong. But the moment you lose focus, or your prompt isn't precise enough, they veer off course—and they do it with total confidence.

Human developers are forced to spend their most precious attention on review—and it's more draining than traditional review. You don't just have to understand the logic; you also have to scrutinize every interface and variable name the model generated.

It's clearly more exhausting than writing the code yourself.

What's more, AI doesn't know the starting point you have in mind. You may have designed a specific interface or method as the beginning of the work—the AI doesn't know that, and doesn't care. Every prompt is a fresh start to it.

So the question of "where the task enters" must be made explicit and enforced—not left to the probabilistic output of an LLM.

As an aside: human fragility around losing the trunk has always existed. It's just that in the past, output was slower, and local drift had time to be corrected naturally. That correction window has now shrunk to nearly zero.

---

## II. Lock Both Ends First, Then Do the Middle

If you've used AI products deeply, you know the pattern: after several rounds of context compression, the AI will systematically—and unintentionally—drift the premises while it works. It will also quietly drift the exit conditions.

It has no intent or awareness of doing this. It's simply producing the optimal continuation based on the current context window, round after round.

In complex tasks, this is as natural to it as breathing. The result: the front doesn't connect, the back doesn't close, and the more work you do in the middle, the more chaotic the whole thing becomes.

This is a fundamental defect of LLM architectures. Current agent compression solutions are either naive truncation, or they preserve everything without focus.

Neither solves the core problem: across multiple turns of conversation, the context becomes a landfill of bytes. The model can't tell what it should be focusing on in the current scenario.

One wrong step leads to another—like the butterfly effect, the starting point and destination both become elusive from the very beginning.

So both ends must be locked. Not the implementation—the contract.

We need to explicitly nail down six things:

- Input
- Output
- State handover
- Definition of completion
- Failure fallback
- Recovery resumption

Once these six are locked, you can implement the middle however you like—it won't escape the interval. Without them locked, the more flexible the middle, the more out of control the whole becomes.

Locking the ends also gives verification a foothold. Without a prior contract, the middle can prove itself correct by redefining the upstream and downstream—something that happens almost daily in AI-assisted development. Once both ends are locked, verification becomes a repeatable judgement, not a debate over interpretations.

---

## III. Points Must Be Upgraded to Nodes

A point in mathematics carries no burden: no responsibility, no state, no verification, no recovery.

In engineering, that doesn't work. Every position that is taken seriously in engineering must be invested with meaning.

So in engineering, a node is five things at once:

- A phase transition point
- A risk isolation point
- A verification checkpoint
- A recovery entry point
- A state handover point

Notice that none of these is "workload."

The purpose of splitting into nodes is not to cut work into pieces for allocation or reporting. It is to separate **risk, responsibility, verification, and recovery**.

If the trunk is a solid block, errors wander around inside it—you don't know which segment is wrong, you don't know where to stop, you don't know where to recover from, and you don't know if you can continue.

Cutting out nodes means driving a row of stakes into the trunk—stakes that you can observe, judge, and say "stop" at.

Can we recursively split forever? No.

For a position to qualify as a node, there are three minimal legitimacy conditions, all mandatory:

1. Removing it would change the trunk's progression logic.
2. It has its own verification checkpoint and closure conditions.
3. After splitting, the inside cannot still be a black box.

If all three are met, it's a node. If even one is missing, it's just a name.

---

## IV. Inside a Node: Five Links

A quick aside:

The inside of an LLM is a black box—that's an indisputable physical fact. This black-box nature cannot be eliminated. But we can manage its size and scope through structure.

Now the main text:

For a node to be valid in engineering, at least five minimal internal conditions must be clearly defined:

1. **Entry**: how to legitimately take over the previous state.
2. **Progression**: what drives it forward.
3. **Failure**: what counts as failure.
4. **Recovery**: how to get back to a state where progression can continue.
5. **Closure**: how to legitimately hand off to the next segment.

Missing any one of these five creates a grey area.

If entry is unclear, takeover is impossible.
If failure is unclear, exceptions contaminate everything.
If recovery is unclear, state becomes chaotic after restart.
If closure is unclear, downstream deadlocks.

One more rule: a node's closure serves the trunk, not itself.

Note:

- Local repair must not contaminate the overall truth.
- Local closure must not fake overall completion.
- Local exceptions must not escalate into system-wide inexplicability.

Nodes can be autonomous, but they cannot become kingdoms unto themselves.

---

## V. Parent and Child—Relative Roles

A parent task is not necessarily a large task.

Here, size does not determine identity—structure does.

If a task itself is a complete line segment—with an input end, an output end, and a lifecycle in between—it is the parent task at that level.

The overall project is certainly the parent task, and the only absolute one: the outermost line segment, with both ends locked and unchangeable. Everything else grows recursively from it.

A child task lives inside a larger segment, contained, constrained, and advanced by that segment's trunk.

Imagine standing in chaos, with nothing but a two-dimensional straight line in front of you. You zoom in—and inside it, you see a complete set of entry, progression, failure, recovery, and closure. At that level, it re-emerges as a parent segment.

The criterion is simple: at its own level, is it a complete, governable, and closeable line segment?

- If yes, it qualifies for upgrade.
- If no, it's just a component within the current parent task, and doesn't need the treatment of an upper layer.

The boundary between parent and child is not drawn by naming.
Not by directory structure. Not by ticket numbers. Not by titles. It's drawn by the answers to five questions:

- Who holds the runtime truth of this segment?
- Who drives the state forward?
- Who defines failure?
- Who is responsible for recovery?
- Who gives the completion judgement?

If these five "who"s are answered clearly, the boundary exists. If not, no amount of naming helps.

In short: "parent" here is a structural state—not a rank.

---

## VI. Where to Stop Splitting

Recursion is not infinite descent.

Each additional layer of splitting brings four new costs:

- Coordination
- Verification
- Closure
- Cognitive burden

---

It also brings benefits, of course:

- More clarity
- Stronger boundaries
- Higher controllability
- Finer recovery granularity

So the question of where to stop is essentially a discrete optimisation problem—both costs and benefits vary with depth, and there is a point of maximum net benefit.

No one expects you to compute that point precisely.

The only requirement: every time you want to split one more layer, you must be able to prove it has net benefit.

If the split only renames things, or is purely for structural neatness—that's negative value, and you must stop.

The stopping point is called a leaf.

A leaf is not "the smallest task." It's "a task where further splitting has no net benefit."

A legitimate leaf must satisfy these conditions:

- Input is clear.
- Output is clear.
- Owner is clear.
- Acceptance checkpoint is clear.
- Failures can be absorbed by the parent layer—no need for a separate recovery layer or internal state machine.

If these are met, stop. If not, no matter how small, it's not a leaf.

Conversely, a task may be large in size—but if these conditions hold, it can still be a leaf.

---

## VII. True Layers vs. Pseudo Layers

Whether in traditional development or AI-assisted development, complex systems have an unavoidable ailment: layer proliferation.

Today a governance layer, tomorrow an orchestration layer, the day after an abstraction layer. More and more names, thicker and thicker structures, with no real gain in control.

When you let AI do refactoring, be especially careful—it has the same enthusiasm for adding layers, and it does it fast and confidently. The criterion for whether an abstraction is legitimate must remain in your hands—not handed over to the AI.

---

#### A true layer must pass three checkpoints.

**First checkpoint:** it must have a new state space—state variables that don't exist in the lower layer, and independent transition rules. The upper layer must be able to make decisions based on this state that the lower layer cannot.

If it only passes through lower-layer variables, it's a wrapper—not a layer.

**Second checkpoint:** it must have new control semantics—it controls dispatching, convergence, degradation, isolation, rollback, release, termination, and other progression logic. It must not simply rename lower-layer actions and repeat them.

**Third checkpoint:** it must have new recovery semantics—it can define its own failures, has its own set of recovery actions, and can handle timeouts, lateness, duplicate execution, and contamination by old attempts. A layer that only propagates exceptions upward does not count.

---

#### Pseudo layers

One final check: the deletion test. Suppose you remove this candidate layer. If the parent layer's judgement, recovery, and control capabilities do not materially change, it's a pseudo layer—remove it. If they do materially change, it's a true layer—keep it in the trunk.

"Looks clearer" is not a benefit. "It hurts to delete it" is.

---

## VIII. Dual Verification

Local completion does not equal overall establishment. This sentence is worth printing on A4 paper, buying a frame, and hanging on the wall.

Child tasks completed, modules run through, tests green—none of it counts.

Anyone who's made it this far, I believe, shares this experience: the tests run, and they report all green. But that green is a self-assessment within its own boundaries—not the closure of the parent task.

A passing test only means "no errors were found within the scope it understands." And whether that scope is actually what you wanted—that, it has no authority to judge.

So verification must happen at two levels.

**Level one: the child-task test gate.** Within its own boundaries, answer: has the contract been fulfilled? Is the state handover correct? Are the recovery conditions satisfied? Without passing this gate, do not proceed to the next step.

**Level two: the parent-task full verification.** Once the child tasks are assembled, are the handover relationships, timing relationships, and overall contract truly closed? This is not duplicated work—this is the final judgement.

If you only do level one, you get a pile of perfect parts and a machine that won't run. If you only do level two, when something breaks you have no local diagnostic capability.

Both layers are required. Neither can be omitted.

From this moment on, the right to advance is determined not by "coding completed," but by "verification passed."

---

## IX. Recovery Semantics

A solution that only defines the success path is not a complete solution.

Real systems will inevitably encounter failure, rollback, timeout, retry, interruption, restart, and contamination by old state. Not defining recovery at design time is equivalent to outsourcing disaster to the future—to that version of yourself who gets woken up in the middle of the night.

Those who develop with AI feel this most acutely: context compression, session interruption, resumption after interruption, parallel writes corrupting state, leftover state from old sessions. You can run into several of these within one or two sessions.

Every node and every segment must answer five questions for its recovery semantics:

- What counts as failure?
- What actions are allowed after failure?
- Based on what evidence do you decide whether to retry or roll back?
- After recovery, how do you reconnect to the trunk?
- How do you prevent old attempts from contaminating the current state after restart?

If these five questions aren't fully answered, recovery is just a slogan.

There's also an iron rule: recovery operates within the boundary of truth. Local recovery must not fake the parent task's completion. Asynchronous recovery must not retroactively rewrite the terminal state of the main task.

Whoever holds the runtime truth holds the final judgement.

Recovery is not "doing everything possible to make things look successful." It is "allowing the system to re-enter a legitimate progression state without tampering with the truth."

These two statements look similar, but they are worlds apart—especially in today's world of increasingly automated recovery.

## X. Human Intervention Takes Priority

From the agricultural revolution seventy thousand years ago to the first industrial revolution, the gap was on the order of tens of thousands of years.
From the first industrial revolution to the second, only a few decades.
Then to the information age—roughly 5–10 years.
Then to the web era—1989 to 2004, about a decade and a half.
Then to web3—2014 to 2021, about 10 years.
And now, the AI age.

The pattern is clear: the speed of technological evolution is accelerating, and the boundaries between these eras are becoming increasingly blurred, overlapping and reinforcing each other.

We may occasionally feel fear about the rapid development of AI. But in my view, that fear is fundamentally a fear of **runaway acceleration**.

How to define the goal, where to set tolerances, whether to make a strategic retreat—these are not things that local automation can legitimately decide, still less an agent. The stronger the capability, the more opportunities there are to overstep, and the more clearly the boundaries must be drawn.

The rule is simple: by default, test gates execute; default blocking executes; default recovery executes. But once human intervention makes a clear ruling, the default process must yield. This is not a negotiation—it yields.

This framework is especially useful for AI agents.

The segment defines the trunk.
Nodes define control points.
Test gates define advancement authorisation.
The true-layer criteria define abstraction legitimacy.
Recovery semantics define the course after failure.
Human-first defines the final decision authority.

This is not a replacement for intelligence. It is a way of drawing a provable, verifiable, rollback-capable, reviewable boundary for intelligence to operate within.

Without this boundary, the stronger the intelligence, the faster it goes out of control.

Human intervention always comes first: technology exists to serve humanity.

---

There is no invention in any of this.

Polaris is currently bootstrapping itself at high speed (it has been running in a single window for about 150 hours, ignoring all dialogue compression, and its chain of thought remains stable). Clearly, it is nearly finished. Otherwise, you would not be seeing the outermost layer of this large methodology.

It's time for us to step out of the traditional development mindset and adopt a new perspective.

We used to deal with features; now we deal with the trunk.
We used to deal with local actions; now we deal with state transitions.
We used to ask "did you do it?"; now we ask "has this line closed the loop?"

At its most basic, it comes down to six questions:

- Where from?
- Where to?
- How to get there?
- Where to stop?
- What to do when something goes wrong?
- How to reconnect when broken?

If these are answered clearly, the segment stands. If not, no matter how much you've done, it remains a fog.

In this article we've clarified many things, but we haven't yet addressed: how do we ensure this system doesn't collapse? That is the subject of the next methodology—and it's simple: only five rules.
